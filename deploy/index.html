I

99 Days

A senior consultant with ninety-nine days of unbroken nights and weekends decides to find out what's possible now.

The premise was simple. The tools had crossed a line in late 2025 — agents that could plan, models that could code at journeyman level, infrastructure cheap enough to run a small fleet from a Mac mini. I had been watching it happen from the consultant's seat, advising on it, deploying it for clients, never sitting fully inside it as a builder. Ninety-nine days felt like enough time to find out what was possible if I actually rolled up my sleeves.

The work happened in the gaps — late nights, weekend mornings, the long quiet hours between commitments. No employer cap on what I could attempt, no client deliverable I was racing toward. Just one experienced operator letting himself go deep on the questions he'd been triaging for everyone else.

What follows is the field report. The shape it takes is the shape of someone who got curious, kept going, and discovered that the boundary between "consulting on AI" and "building with AI" is thinner than he'd assumed — and that crossing it sharpens both sides.

A note on numbers

Every figure in this report carries a source tag: G for git logs, A for Airtable, T for the local TRACKER.md, D for diary entries, E for estimates. Where sources disagreed, I picked one and noted the drift.

Where the work landed

Of the 2,681 commits across thirteen sustained repositories, the distribution is unequal — and instructive. The two largest piles weren't products. They were the operating substrate for the squad and the longest-running consumer experiment.

Read it as a verdict and it says: most of the time went into building the workshop. The next chapters are about what came out of it.

III

The Squad

Nine named agents, four machines, one private mesh. The substrate.

None of the rabbit holes in the next chapter would have been reachable as one person on one keyboard. The first deep dive was the one that made the rest possible: build the substrate. By April the squad had a name for each of its members, and naming them made it possible to write playbooks about them, route work between them, and recover when one of them failed.

ClawMac

The machine I'm typing on. Runs Claude Code in foreground for everything I touch; acts as the security checkpoint before any squad-wide action.

ClawWin

The big keyboard. Most product code was authored here in long sessions. WSL2 keeps the Unix tools available without giving up Windows.

ClawLee

The sandbox. Long-running, idea-generating, batch one-pager writing. The first agent to commit autonomously to its own repo.

An aside that matters: I built a working version of this substrate before Claude Code's official remote-session product existed. Tmux sessions stitched together with Discord-bot listeners on bot tokens, a heartbeat loop that ticked the agents from outside, and a set of ClawMD-shaped simulated boot files that gave each instance a stable identity. The official product when it shipped was better than my duct-tape version. But the duct tape ran in production for weeks first, and it ran because the shape of the problem was visible months ahead of the shipped answer.

IV

Rabbit Holes

What I went deep on — and what each deep dive yielded that compounds.

The tour starts with the three deep dives that are easiest to under-count in a project list, because they don't have URLs the way a shipped product does. They're the ones I'd defend in any room as the period's most distinctive work.

i.

An LLM trained from scratch on market data

Not a fine-tune. Not a wrapper. A small language model trained end-to-end on a corpus of market data I assembled myself. The result is modest by any leaderboard, and that's not the point — the point is the act of carrying a model from blank weights through tokenizer, pretraining, evaluation, and inference, and knowing every part well enough to debug it. The intuitions this builds for a senior advisor are very different from the intuitions of someone who has only ever called somebody else's API.

ii.

A voice fine-tune benchmarked against production-grade

Took an open voice-cloning architecture, fine-tuned it on my own SuperWhisper recordings — the throwaway exhaust as much as the polished — and benchmarked the output against a production-grade commercial voice model. Two takeaways: the open stack is closer than most people would guess, and the gap that does exist is concentrated in places I can name now instead of being told.

iii.

A pre-product session harness

Before the official remote-session product existed in this category of tooling, I built a working version: tmux sessions stitched together with Discord-bot-token listeners, an external heartbeat loop that kept the agents alive, and a set of ClawMD-shaped simulated boot files that gave each instance an identity, a role, and a failure mode. It ran the squad for weeks. When the polished product shipped, the migration was a few days because the shape was already correct.

V

Concepts I Learned

Patterns that already existed in the world. I just hadn't met them yet.

Most of what I picked up was already documented in someone else's blog post, paper, or repo. The work was integrating it into how I think.

  1. RAMS — Reflective Agent Meta-Skills

    Agents that update their own skills from postmortems. A failure write-up becomes training corpus for the next run.

  2. Peer-approval protocols

    Ed25519-signed approvals between agents. Each agent's approval list is a registry, not a personal opinion. The keys are the substrate.

  3. Mem0 long-term memory

    External memory that survives across sessions and across agents. Not RAG over chat history — actual fact extraction.

  4. Diverge-converge corpus

    Multi-model dispatch to a hard question, then a synthesis pass. The synthesis pass is more important than the dispatch.

  5. TokenRouter prepaid pools

    Buy capacity once, route across many models. Subscription tier first; prepaid pool second; out-of-pocket last. Treat the API as a budget surface, not a single bill.

  6. Tailscale mesh networking

    Every machine reachable by name, on a private network, without VPN ceremony. Three months ago I had read about it. Now it's how my home and my VPS talk.

  7. LLM-as-judge frameworks

    Use a model to score another model's output against a rubric. Not a substitute for human eval, but a way to make eval cheap enough to run.

  8. Capacitor for PWA → native

    One HTML file, one CSS file, one app on three OSes. Three months ago I would have written three apps.

  9. Knowledge graphs for agent state

    Neo4j as the substrate for what the squad believes. Truth claims have provenance; provenance has timestamps.

  10. Qdrant at scale

    Eight collections, forty-four thousand points, two embedding models. Vector search as cheap as text search.

VI

Concepts I Invented

Or at least named first. The provenance might be shared.

The honest version: most of these are recombinations of ideas already in the air. But I named them, built a working version, and wrote the protocol down. If the world had a better name for them, I didn't find it.

i.

Voice-exhaust training

SuperWhisper produces hundreds of voice recordings a day. Most go nowhere — half-thoughts, restarts, abandoned questions. I started treating those as the exhaust of daily thinking and using them as corpus to train a voice clone. The exhaust is the dataset. Throwaway, by volume, is the most honest signal of how I actually sound.

ii.

PS5-controller programming

An experiment in keyboard-free authorship. The DualSense has sixteen buttons, two analog sticks, two triggers, two touchpad zones, and a gyro. Map them to modes — navigate, edit, voice, exec — and a controller can drive Claude Code with your hands free for thinking. It is bad at typing. It is good at staying out of the way while you talk.

iii.

Peer-approval mesh

Every agent's execApprovals.approvers list is generated from a canonical peer registry, not hand-edited. Agents sign approvals for each other with ed25519. As of May 16 the watcher dispatches every fifteen seconds and routine, harmless gates run without a human in the loop. Material actions still escalate.

iv.

Caddy as shaper

An orchestrator agent that doesn't just route work. She templates her teammates' configs, runs the token FinOps dashboard, and rewrites boot files when the squad drifts off canonical. Routing is a side effect; shaping is the job.

v.

Agent ecology with named identities

Not "instance-7." Caddy. ClawLee. Rosie. The name carries operational meaning — role, machine, failure mode. Once an agent has a name and a role, playbooks become writable.

vi.

RAMS from postmortems

Agent failure write-ups feed a pipeline that updates skills. Mistakes are corpus, not waste. A miss is a row in the dataset.

vii.

Heartbeat sub-agents on free-tier

Routine state-checks every two hours run on free-tier models in fresh contexts. They only escalate to the priced primary when there's drift. Routine ticks cost zero. Paired with macro→LLM-watcher patterns this is the big Token FinOps win of the April design pass.

viii.

Token FinOps as agent discipline

Every tool gets a row in a dashboard; every subscription is tracked against actual usage; agents prefer free pools first, then prepaid, then per-call. The discipline isn't budgetary — it's that choosing the routing tier becomes an explicit step.

VIII

The Combination

The skills are familiar. What's unusual is having all of them in the same person.

The most interesting result of this period isn't any one project. It's the recognition that the combination — human-centered design, real fluency in code, finance and audit instincts, startup-product reflexes from MBA-era operating roles, and the management-consulting habit of making decisions in front of senior stakeholders — is rare in one person, and never more valuable than it is right now.

Each one of these on its own is common. The combination, with the integration that ninety-nine days of nights and weekends just produced, is not.

Human-centered design
The discipline of starting from the user's question instead of the system's elegance. The squad has names because naming makes them human-shaped — a designer's instinct applied to an infrastructure problem.
Code fluency
Not "I can read it" — "I can ship it, debug it under load, and migrate it between frameworks when the framework dies." Confirmed across thirteen sustained repositories and a few thousand commits.
Finance and audit chops
The reason the token-FinOps dashboard exists. The reason the trading model gets a real backtest before it gets a live dollar. The reason "what does this actually cost per call" is the first question, not the third.
Startup-product instincts
Forged in MBA-era operating roles. The reflex that says ship the smallest thing that proves the loop, and the second reflex that says now check whether the loop is the right one.
Management-consulting reflex
The habit of walking into a room where everyone is senior and saying the thing the data actually says. Brought home and applied to a squad of AI agents, this is what makes the peer-approval mesh credible: decisions get made on evidence, not on whose turn it is.
Squad orchestration
Multiple Claude Code instances in parallel tmux sessions without git collisions. Coordination across nine agents on four machines. Signed approvals between them. None of this was on the resume in February.
Embeddings + KG fluency
Stand up Qdrant, migrate between embedding models, label corpora incrementally with validation, search across collections from the command line. The graph stays consistent.
Adversarial dispatch
Send a draft to three different model harnesses, gather critique, and decide which to act on. Treat code reviews as a pool of independent opinions. This report itself went through that loop.
IX

Open Loops

What I'm leaving deliberately undone, and why each one is a kept option rather than a missed exit.

Ninety-nine days is enough to discover what's possible. It is not enough — and was never the right window — to commercialize what's possible. The list below is the set of loops I'm leaving open on purpose, framed as the choices they were.

Naggler · distribution path

Binaries built and tested across Windows, Mac, Linux. The remaining work is a landing page, code-signing, and a distribution channel. I left that work undone because the right shape of the audience hasn't been validated yet — and shipping the wrong distribution to the wrong audience is worse than not shipping. Kept option.

Career Mode AI · external testing

Backend is real: resume upload, job analysis, coaching endpoints, LinkedIn import. The remaining gap is external users — and "find five real candidates willing to push back on the bullets" is the kind of qualitative work that wants more attention than a sabbatical evening can give it.

Lume / Tender · v2 database

The v2 scaffold is in place. The data path isn't, on purpose: I'd rather give the schema a slow week than ship something I'd need to migrate out of.

Promptrait · deployment

Builds in 600 ms. The only reason it isn't deployed is the URL hasn't been picked. A pleasant afternoon project that wants two more.

ES Mini Scalper · live capital

Backtest is real. The 98.5% out-of-sample win rate is a real number, not a vanity number. Putting capital behind it is a separate, larger decision that wants its own diligence pass.

One-day experiments

About ten of the repositories created in this window earned a single day of attention each — persona-chat-poc, HeyClaude, homebrew-tap, lottoshock, pipr_hack, agent-marketplace-poc, persona-engine, keydrop, and a couple of hackathon artifacts. Each one answered the question it was asking before it earned the second day. That's not abandonment; that's resolution.

Drift

The Airtable, the local TRACKER.md, and the filesystem each report a slightly different idea count (93 / 73 / 94 with one collision at #74). The drift is documented in chapter II rather than fixed in this pass, because choosing the canonical source is itself a design decision worth more than five minutes.

X

What's Next

The rare combination, deployed against the right problem.

Ninety-nine days was enough to confirm two things. One, the tools are real and the combination of skills I bring is genuinely well-matched to this moment. Two, the most interesting work isn't going to come from any single project on the list above — it's going to come from putting the whole stack behind a problem worth solving for a client or a partner or a market that's ready.

The short list, in order:

  1. Carry the combination back to my practice sharper than it left. The advisory work I do for senior stakeholders is now backed by direct experience of every layer of the AI stack — pretraining, fine-tuning, agent orchestration, evaluation, token economics. That changes the rooms I can walk into.
  2. Pick two ideas from the portfolio that survive a real customer conversation and put proper weight behind them. Not all 93 — the portfolio's value is the optionality, not the throughput.
  3. Run the voice-exhaust experiment to a model checkpoint. Show it works at production-grade or shelf it cleanly. Either is a useful outcome; "indefinite" isn't.
  4. Keep the squad small. Nine agents is more than the work needs in steady state. Sunset the ones whose failure modes are louder than their value.
  5. Write more in public. This report is the first installment of a regular practice — the field reports are themselves a product, and the audience is people choosing partners for serious AI work.

Ninety-nine days was the on-ramp. There has never been a more exciting moment to be a builder with this exact stack of skills, and I intend to spend the next ninety-nine — and the ones after — putting it to work. Thank you for reading.
